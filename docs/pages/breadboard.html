Two-node test rig · breadboard layout

Early-warning breadboard

The rig from the test plan, on two half-size breadboards: a camera node with one nRF (the trigger board) and a PIR node 20 m away. The camera's verdict goes to the trigger nRF, which writes everything to a USB serial log, and to the MeshCore stack (XIAO nRF52840 plus Wio-SX1262), which sits beside the breadboard on its own USB and joins last. Until the two new XIAO nRF52840 boards arrive from Seeed, the trigger and PIR nodes run on boards borrowed from the LoRa stacks; re-flash MeshCore on those before putting them back.

Charging the cells

The Adafruit bq24074 charger (ID 4755) charges the cells off the breadboard. Plug a cell's JST lead into its battery socket and power it from USB-C. It charges at 1 A by default (500 mA or 1.5 A by solder jumper), against about 100 mA from the XIAO's own charger, so a 2200 mAh cell takes a few hours instead of about a day. Charge both cells before the first run. It also has a JST load socket that can power a node while a cell tops up, and the same board is the charger in the rev B carrier design.

Before the first power-up

  • Cell and protection. The 1781 cells may already have their own protection; read the label. If you use a bare cell in an owned wire-lead holder instead, put a 1S protection board between the cell and the rails (cell to B+ and B−, load to P+ and P−; the XIAO BAT− goes to P−). First check the board on the bench with a current-limited supply and a meter: the overcharge, overdischarge and overcurrent cutoffs, and the idle drain with the microamp meter. Do not stack a protection board on a cell that already has one.
  • Polarity. Check the JST breakout's + and − marking against the cell's red and black wires.
  • MiniBoost EN. With the boost alone on the rails, check whether EN floats high or low and read the output with a meter before the S3 is connected.

What the firmware does on this rig

  • PIR node: sends a trigger packet on motion, and a heartbeat every few seconds. Each packet carries a sequence number.
  • Trigger nRF: logs every packet with its time, sequence number and RSSI, raises EN on a trigger, then logs the camera's boot message, first frame and verdict from the UART line, and drops EN at the end of the window.
  • Camera: boots when the boost comes up, runs detection, and prints its boot, frame and verdict messages on D6.

Bring-up order

  1. Charge both cells. Then the PIR node alone: flash it, check the PIR output on D0, and measure sleep and transmit current.
  2. Camera node, boost only: build the rails, the boost and the Schottky (wires 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 9). With a meter, check the output is 0 V with EN low and about 5 V with EN high.
  3. Add the trigger nRF and EN (wires 10, 12), flash it, and confirm it receives the PIR node's packets and switches the boost.
  4. Add the camera (wires 4, 5, 11), flash the timing firmware, and read the boot-to-verdict log.
  5. IR test: flash the camera_view env, open the live view, and point a TV remote at the lens while pressing a button. A bright purple-white spot means the camera passes near-IR and an 850 nm lamp will work; if not, a NoIR camera module is needed. Do this before buying any lamp.
  6. Setup mode: with the camera on USB, add jumper 14 (camera 3V3 to D1), reset, join the ew-camera Wi-Fi network (the 2.4 GHz antenna must be fitted) and open http://192.168.4.1/. Check the settings page, the live view and the session log, then remove the jumper.
  7. Move the PIR node out to 20 m for the link tests.
  8. MeshCore stack last: fit the LoRa antenna first (never power the SX1262 without one), add wires 15 and 16, and power the stack from its own USB. It stays on stock MeshCore until the verdict-to-channel-message hook is written; for now confirm it receives the camera's UART lines.

Left out of this rig

  • MeshCore firmware hook. The stack is wired in but the code that turns the UART verdict into a channel message is not written. It also stays on USB power here; in the field it runs from the cell and is always on.
  • IR gate. The trigger nRF switches the lamp together with the boost EN, so it needs a spare trigger-nRF pin, a logic-level MOSFET and a small trigger-node firmware change that is not written yet. Do it after the IR test and once the detection chain works.
  • SETUP/RUN switch. The carrier will have a slide switch from the trigger board's 3V3 to a SETUP node (a Schottky into the boost EN and 10 k to camera D1). On the bench the jumper stands in for it.
  • Charger and solar on the board. The bq24074 charges the cells on its own, off the breadboard. It joins the carrier in rev B, with solar.

Worked out from the design notes, not from datasheets. The XIAO pin orientation is derived from the module rotation (USB left or right), and the 0.6 inch pin-row spacing and the MiniBoost pin order (IN, GND, OUT, EN) are assumptions. Check these against the real boards before powering anything. Also check whether the MiniBoost's EN pin floats high or low by itself, since an nRF pin that is not driven yet could switch it. The cells are protected 18650s with a JST lead, so check the breakout's + and − marking against the cell's red and black wires before connecting.
